A classifier gives probabilities; a business needs decisions. A profit curve picks the decision threshold by what each kind of right and wrong answer is worth, instead of by accuracy.

## Example: who gets a retention discount?

The data is the [Telco Customer Churn](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/blastchar/telco-customer-churn) dataset, where the target is whether a customer leaves. An XGBoost classifier scores a ROC AUC of 0.815 on the test set.

Choosing a threshold from the distribution of predictions alone is not obvious. Confusion matrices at several thresholds show the trade-off, but not which side of it to prefer.

## The payoff matrix

A **payoff matrix** puts a value on each cell of the confusion matrix. Suppose a discount offered to a customer who would have churned keeps them, which is worth £200, and a discount offered to one who would have stayed anyway is £80 wasted. Not offering a discount costs nothing either way.

At a threshold of 0.5 the model correctly targets 189 churners and wrongly targets 179 non-churners, so

$$
189 \times 200 - 179 \times 80 = 37{,}800 - 14{,}320 = \pounds 23{,}480
$$

of value on the test set.

## The profit curve

Was 0.5 the best threshold? Compute the profit at every threshold:

```python
def profit_curve(y_true, y_pred, payoff_matrix, n_points: int = 101):
    thresholds = np.linspace(0, 1, n_points)
    profits = []
    for threshold in thresholds:
        cm = confusion_matrix(y_true, (y_pred >= threshold).astype(int))
        profits.append((cm * payoff_matrix.T).sum())
    return thresholds, profits
```

The best threshold is 0.22, which makes £32,360: about 38% more than at 0.5. Because a retained churner is worth far more than a wasted discount costs, it pays to cast the net wide.

## Limitations

- It is only as good as the payoff matrix. Meaningless payoffs give a meaningless curve.
- It assumes all customers are worth about the same.
- It applies to classification decisions like churn / no churn. For continuous decisions, see [Continuous Outcomes](/research/machine-learning/decision-optimisation/continuous-outcomes).

## Different thresholds for different customers

When some customers are worth much more than others, it makes sense to target them more aggressively. Suppose keeping a customer is worth three times their monthly charge, while a wasted discount still costs £80. Split customers into four bands by monthly charge (up to £20, £20 to £60, £60 to £100, over £100), give each band a payoff matrix based on its average charge, and find each band's best threshold:

| Band      | Best threshold |
| --------- | :------------: |
| Low       |      0.45      |
| Medium    |      0.64      |
| High      |      0.19      |
| Very high |      0.07      |

The highest-value customers get the lowest thresholds, since losing them costs the most. (The medium band is an exception, worth a closer look.) Using per-band thresholds totals £44,919.

The original notebook compared that with the £32,360 from a single threshold, but the two use different payoffs: £200 per retained customer in one case, three times the monthly charge in the other. The fair comparison is with the best _single_ threshold under the per-band payoffs, which is the peak of the total curve above. Per-band thresholds can never do worse than that, and the gap is the real value of segmenting.
